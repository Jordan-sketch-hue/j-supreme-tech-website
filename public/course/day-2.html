The Big Idea

Almost every constraint you're operating under is borrowed. Someone else hit a wall, accepted it, and you inherited their ceiling.

First-principles reasoning is the discipline of going the other direction: stripping every problem or assumption down to the things that are undeniably true — not analogies, not conventions, not what similar businesses do — and reasoning back up from there to a new conclusion. It's the operating tool behind every genuinely novel move.

The reason it's rare isn't that it's hard. It's that reasoning by analogy is faster and usually good enough — and the brain defaults to it. "Similar agencies charge around $X." "That kind of build usually takes Y weeks." "No one in this market has done that." Those statements are always observations about the past, dressed up as facts about the present. First-principles asks whether they're actually true — and most of the time, under examination, they're not.

"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."

— Richard Feynman · physicist

The Assumption Machine

How analogical thinking becomes a ceiling

Analogical (fast)

"X works like Y because it looks similar." Draws from memory and pattern. Fast, efficient, usually correct — and entirely dependent on whether the pattern actually applies here.

First Principles (powerful)

"What do I know is actually true here?" Identifies the bedrock. Slower, effortful — and the only path to a genuinely different outcome.

The trap isn't that analogy is wrong. Most of the time it's right, and you should use it. The trap is applying it to the decisions where it's building a false floor under your thinking — especially pricing, positioning, what's possible, and how long things take. Those four are where inherited ceilings cost the most.

Seen In The Wild

The SpaceX moment — and yours

  • SpaceX, 2001. Every aerospace company quoted Musk $65M+ per rocket launch. The analogical answer: that's what rockets cost, buy one. The first-principles question: what is a rocket made of? Aerospace-grade aluminum, titanium, carbon fiber, copper, electronics — available on commodity markets for roughly 2–3% of the quoted price. The rest was manufacturing margin and institutional overhead. SpaceX didn't reinvent physics. They took the same materials and removed the inherited cost structure.
  • Your pricing. "JST charges $X" or "competitors are at $Y" — that's analogical pricing. What is your service actually made of? Labor (time), tooling (AI-compressed effort), expertise (compounded skill). What does each unit actually cost you to deliver, and what outcome does it produce for the client? That math gives a number with no reference to competitors — and usually produces a different answer in both directions.
  • Your build timeline. "A site like this takes 4–6 weeks" is an analogy. What does it actually require? Design decisions (async, done in hours with the right brief), development (which components already exist?), client feedback loops (the real variable). Strip it and you may find the true bottleneck is a single waiting period — and that the rest compresses.
Real talk

Most "I can't charge more" beliefs aren't financial facts — they're social ones. Someone comparable didn't charge more, and you inherited their hesitation. Strip it: what does the outcome actually produce for the client? What would it cost them to not have it? That math has nothing to do with what a competitor charged. Start there.

The Process

Five moves — tap each to expand

1Identify the constraint or question precisely
Don't start with "how do we fix this?" Start with "what exactly is the constraint?" Vague problems produce vague first principles. The more specific the question, the sharper the reasoning. Write it as a sentence.
2List every assumption baked into it
Ask: "Why does this seem true?" Keep asking. You're hunting for statements like "because everyone does it this way" or "because that's what it costs." Those are assumptions, not truths. List them all explicitly — they become visible for the first time.
3Strike the borrowed ones
For each assumption: is this undeniably true, or is it inherited from someone else's experience/context? Strike anything borrowed. You're not destroying them — you're testing whether they actually apply here. Most don't survive this step unchanged.
4State what you know is actually true
What's left after the strike? The bedrock — the things that would be true regardless of context, convention, or what anyone else has done. These are your actual inputs. Physical limits. Actual cost structure. Real buyer psychology. Write them.
5Build back up from there
Now construct your solution, price, timeline, or position from the truths — not from the assumptions you just stripped. The result usually looks different from what everyone else is doing. That's not a warning sign. That's the point.
The Threads

Where this plugs in

The reason Systems Thinking (L1) came first is that first-principles reasoning operates on the structure of a problem, not its events. When you stripped SpaceX's launch cost to raw materials, you were finding the actual stocks and flows — not reacting to the sticker price event. The two tools work together: systems thinking shows you the structure; first principles strips that structure to bedrock.

In the lessons ahead: Expected Value (L3) uses first principles to define what the real inputs to a bet actually are. Decision Hygiene (L10) formalises the pre-mortem as an explicit assumption-audit before you commit. And when we get to Pricing Power (L27), this will return — because pricing from first principles produces a fundamentally different position than pricing by analogy.

The question that cuts

"What would have to be true for this to work?" is almost always more productive than "why won't this work?" The second question ends at the first obstacle. The first question identifies whether the obstacle is a law of physics or an inherited assumption — and those are very different things.

🛠 Operator's Field Note · Apple Developer Program

Before an app ships on iOS it must pass through a platform review process — which is itself a first-principles system: Apple doesn't ask whether your app is good, it asks whether it satisfies specific binary rules. Knowing the rules exactly lets you build to them, not discover them post-rejection. As of mid-2026 (Apple revises continuously — confirm before submitting):

  • $99/year for an individual or organisation Apple Developer Program membership. Required for any production TestFlight or App Store release. Enroll at developer.apple.com.
  • Certificates & provisioning profiles — Apple signs every build. You need a Distribution Certificate (generated in Keychain) and an App Store provisioning profile (generated in App Store Connect) before Xcode can archive for distribution. If one expires, builds stop shipping.
  • App Store Connect roles — Account Holder, Admin, Developer, Marketing, Sales. If you're handing access to a contractor, give them Developer — not Admin. Admin can revoke your own access.
  • Bundle ID — set once, never changes. Uses reverse-domain notation: online.jsupremetech.appname. Changing it creates a new app in App Store Connect.
  • App Privacy nutrition labels — you self-declare what data the app collects and how it's used. These must match the actual code — reviewers check. Mismatch = rejection or removal.

First-principles lens: the 3-7 day review window isn't random — it's a balancing loop Apple built to keep the store quality above a threshold. Design your submission to satisfy the loop's rules on first entry, not through iteration. One clean submission beats three rushed rejections. (Not legal advice.)

Carry This

Almost every constraint is a borrowed assumption wearing the costume of a fact. The question isn't "why can't this be done?" — it's "what would have to be true for this to work?" Strip the problem to undeniable truths — physical limits, actual cost structures, real human needs — then build back up from there. Anything that falls in between is negotiable. Most limits are priced-in history, not physics. Check before you accept them.
