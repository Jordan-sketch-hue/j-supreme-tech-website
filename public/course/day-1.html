The Big Idea

Most people manage their work one event at a time — a deal falls through, a launch underperforms, a number dips — and they fight each fire as it appears.

The people who compound do something different: they look past the events to the structure underneath, because the same structure keeps producing the same kind of events no matter how many fires you put out. Strip away the jargon and any system — a company, a market, a body, a habit — is built from just three things:

  • Stocks — what accumulates. Cash, trust, subscribers, skill, reputation. Anything you could snapshot and measure right now.
  • Flows — the rates that fill or drain a stock. Revenue in, expenses out. Customers in, churn out. The verbs to the stock's nouns.
  • Feedback loops — what you get when a stock's level loops back to change its own flows. Where systems get their power, and their surprises.
Two Loops Run Everything

Snowballs and thermostats

Reinforcing

More leads to more. Money compounds; a trusted brand earns more trust. How empires and bankruptcies are built — exponential in both directions.

Balancing

Resists change, pulls toward a target. A thermostat, hunger, a market reverting to its mean, a risk limit that refuses the next bad trade. How systems stay stable.

And the most underrated force of all: delays — the gap between an action and its result. You post for weeks before traction; you cut spend and sales sag a month later. Delays are why people over-correct, panic, and oscillate — acting as if feedback were instant when it never is.

Why It's The Operator's Lens

You don't really run a company — you run a system of systems, every product line and team its own web of stocks and flows. The leap from operator to owner is the moment you stop fighting events one by one and start redesigning the structure that produces them.

A founder who personally closes every sale has built an event. A founder who builds a referral engine — happy customers producing more customers — has built a loop. The first needs you forever; the second compounds while you sleep. Ask it of everything: is this an event, or a loop?

Seen In The Wild

The same shape, everywhere

  • Amazon's flywheel — lower prices bring customers, attract sellers, widen selection, improve experience, bring more customers. Bezos sketched it on a napkin.
  • A bank run — the identical loop turned malignant: fear drives withdrawals, which cause real insolvency, which deepen the fear.
  • A drawdown spiral (educational, not advice) — a loss shrinks the account, so the same % risk is fewer dollars to recover with, while stress raises risk-taking. A pre-set limit is an installed balancing loop that breaks it. Discipline is engineering, not personality.
Real talk

Most client-work chaos is a loop problem wearing an event costume. A client ghosts, a launch underperforms, a team member keeps missing deadlines — and you keep reacting to each one individually. The loop is the thing: an unclear scope flow, a trust deficit, a missing accountability structure. Same structure, same events. Find the loop first.

Leverage Points

Where to actually push

Donella Meadows ranked the places you can intervene in a system, weakest to strongest. The trap: the obvious, satisfying moves are the weak ones — and everyone crowds there. (tap each)

Tweak the numbersWeak
A 5% discount, a bigger budget, one more feature. Everyone competes here and it barely moves the system.
Change the loopsStronger
Add a referral mechanic. Install a hard risk limit. Automate a flow so it runs without a human. You're editing the structure now, not its dials.
Change the goal & paradigmStrongest
What is the system actually for? Shifting from "I sell services" to "I build assets that compound" silently rewires every decision beneath it. Almost no one operates here.
The quiet truth about leverage

The highest-leverage move is usually small and invisible — a rule, a default, a changed incentive — not a louder version of the effort everyone else is making. Find the structural one.

Loop or Event?

You tell me

Read each situation. Decide before you tap. No penalty — just pattern recognition.

Three scenarios

You've been posting daily content for 3 weeks straight. Engagement is flat. Then in week 4 — traction. Comments, shares, new follows arriving daily.

Loop. The audience trust stock and algorithm signal were building invisibly the whole time. The "traction moment" was the overflow — not a sudden change, but the accumulated stock finally crossing a threshold. Delays hid the compounding from you.

A vendor sent you the wrong invoice amount. Overcharged by $200. First time this has happened.

Event. An isolated billing error with no structural cause. Fix it, flag it, move on. Don't build a system around a one-off. (If it happens again next month — that's a different answer.)

A client paid late. Again. Third time this quarter. Different reason each time.

Loop. Three different reasons is the tell — it's never really about the reason. There's a structural dynamic: unclear payment terms, no real consequence for late payment, or the client's own cash flow loop. The event is the late payment; the loop is the relationship structure. Fix the structure, not the excuses.
🛠 Operator's Field Note · clearing App Store review

A review process is itself a balancing loop — it exists to reject anything off-spec. Don't react after each rejection; design to its rules up front. As of mid-2026 (Apple revises its guidelines, so confirm before submitting):

  • Completeness — a working demo account, no broken or empty states. "It does nothing" is an instant no.
  • Permissions — a clear purpose string for each; no login wall unless the app is genuinely account-based.
  • Account deletion — if users can sign up, they must be able to delete in-app, not via "contact us."
  • Privacy labels that actually match what you collect.

Structural fix: one reusable pre-submit checklist across all your apps, so the same loop never bites twice. (Not legal advice.)

Creative Stack

Photography · Editing · Videography

The language gap between what you picture and what gets delivered is almost always a vocabulary problem — not a talent problem.

You know what you want. The issue is that photographers, videographers, and editors operate in a shared technical dialect that most clients never learn. Once you know the terms, your briefs become specs — and specs cut revision cycles the same way a clear system structure cuts rework. Each term below is one you can use tomorrow.

Photography
Hero shot
The single primary image — the "face" of a campaign, page, or ad. Brief: "I need a hero shot for the website header."
Lifestyle shot
Subject in a real, chosen context. The environment matters as much as the product. Implies casting, location scouting.
Flat lay
Overhead arrangement of objects on a surface. Standard for e-commerce and product social.
Bokeh
The aesthetic background blur from a shallow depth of field. "Heavy bokeh" = subject sharp, everything else melted. Signals premium.
Catchlight
The light reflection in a subject's eyes. Its absence makes portraits feel flat. Brief: "Make sure we have catchlight."
RAW files
Unprocessed sensor data — maximum post-production flexibility. Always ask for RAWs alongside delivered JPEGs.
Culling
Sorting through all images to select the best. "Send the culled selects" = your edited picks, not everything shot.
Moodboard
A reference collection (images, ads, editorial) that defines the style and tone you want. Non-negotiable in every brief. Send it before the shoot, not after.
300 DPI
Resolution for print. Web/screen: 72-96 DPI. Always specify the intended use so the photographer delivers the right format.
Videography
A-roll
Primary footage — interview, on-camera speaking, main subject action. The spine of the edit.
B-roll
Supplementary coverage — environment, product close-ups, cutaways. Without it, an editor has nowhere to cut.
Shot list
A pre-planned list of specific shots to capture on the day. Write this before every shoot. "Document the event" is not a shot list.
Coverage
Getting enough angles and takes that the editor has options. "We didn't get coverage" means you're stuck with what you have.
Gimbal
Motorized stabilizer — smooth, cinematic movement without a dolly track. Handheld without one reads as intentionally raw.
24fps
The cinematic frame rate. Gives the "movie feel." Use 60fps if you want slow-motion capability in the edit (play back at 30fps = 2× slow-mo).
Talking head
On-camera interview or statement setup. Standard for testimonials, brand messaging, explainer content.
Aspect ratio
16:9 = YouTube / landscape. 9:16 = Reels / Stories. 4:5 = feed portrait. 1:1 = square post. Specify this upfront — it determines framing decisions on the day.
Run-and-gun
Minimal gear, single operator, fast-moving. Documentary feel. Signals authenticity, not budget cuts — if that's intentional.
Editing
J-cut / L-cut
The audio from the next (J) or previous (L) clip overlaps the video cut. Makes edits feel seamless instead of abrupt. "I want smooth J-cuts throughout."
LUT
Look Up Table — a one-click color grade preset. "Apply a warm cinematic LUT" is a complete instruction.
Color correction vs grading
Correction = technical fix (exposure, balance). Grading = creative treatment (mood, palette). They're two separate steps.
VO
Voice over. Narration recorded separately and laid over the visuals. "I need VO on this" is clear. "I need narration" sometimes isn't.
Ducking
Automatically lowering background music when dialogue or VO plays. Standard in any polished edit — if it's not there, you hear it instantly.
H.264 / H.265
The standard export codecs for web and social. H.265 = same quality, roughly half the file size. "Export H.264 MP4, 1080p" is a complete delivery spec.
Deliverables
The specific final files you're paying for. Define them explicitly: "10 edited JPEGs at 300 DPI + web copies + original RAWs. One 60s Reel cut 9:16 + a 16:9 version."
Revisions
"Revisions" = changes to the edit from existing footage. "Re-shoot" = going back on location. Make this distinction explicit in every contract or the line blurs.
How to Brief a Creative — 8 Rules
1Send a moodboard first. 5–10 reference images or videos that show the vibe, tone, and feel. One moodboard replaces a paragraph of adjectives.
2Name the platform and placement. "9:16 for Instagram Reels, 30 seconds, hook must land in first 3 seconds" is a brief. "A video for social" is not.
3State the style register. "Commercial polished" vs "documentary raw" vs "cinematic moody" vs "UGC-style organic." These produce completely different outputs.
4Name your color direction. "Warm, earthy tones — amber, terracotta, cream" vs "cool and clinical — white, grey-blue" vs "on-brand colors only: [hex codes]."
5Write a shot list for video. Not "capture the event" — "wide establishing of the storefront, medium on the handshake, ECU on the product label, talking head 1 min to camera."
6Separate corrections from revisions. State upfront how many rounds are included and what each covers. "Two rounds of revisions on the edit; re-shoots are a separate fee."
7Specify deliverables exactly. File format, resolution, codec, color space, quantity. Don't leave it open — you'll get whatever's easiest to export.
8Give a hard deadline, not "ASAP." "Delivered by [date], final files by [date + 2 days]" in the contract. Urgency without a date is noise.

"A system is never the sum of its parts; it is the product of their interactions."

— Russell Ackoff · systems scientist

Carry This

Whatever accumulates is a stock; whatever changes it is a flow; when a stock changes its own flows you have a loop — reinforcing if it snowballs, balancing if it seeks a target — and delays are why people over-react to both. Empires are reinforcing loops with patience; ruin is a reinforcing loop without a brake. Install the right loops; push the high-leverage points.
